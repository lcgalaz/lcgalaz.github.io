**Banco de México in the Postwar Era: A Decade of Successive Adjustments (1945–1954)**
{: .paper__title}

*Chapter 3 in [Cien años del Banco de México: una breve historia](https://www.libros.unam.mx/gd-cien-anos-del-banco-de-mexico-una-breve-historia-9786076420454-libro.html){:target="_blank"}. UNAM, 2025. With Gerardo Esquivel.*

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>
<div class="abstract" markdown="1">

The chapter reconstructs how the Bank of Mexico navigated three implicit and often incompatible mandates (industrial development, defending the parity committed to the Fund, and containing domestic prices) through reserve requirements, selective credit allocation, direct lending to the federal government, and two devaluations. Drawing on the Bank's annual reports, IMF documentation, and the addresses of successive finance ministers at the national banking conventions, we trace the 1948 float, the June 1949 realignment at 8.65 pesos (which preceded the September 1949 sterling-led devaluation wave), and the coordinated 1954 decision that fixed the parity held until 1976. Whether the June 1949 timing reflects technical anticipation or a delayed response to an imbalance already visible in 1946 remains open, though the sequence itself is documented. We also propose a reconsideration of Carlos Novoa, the least studied governor and the only one drawn from private banking. His 1952 essay on economic planning places him within the same developmentalist current as Villaseñor and Rodrigo Gómez. This bears on his intellectual placement, not on the separate claim that the Bank's operational conduct rested with Gómez. What endured was a learned capacity to adjust without abandoning the investment agenda, which framed the terms of the later Desarrollo Estabilizador.

**Sources.** Bank of Mexico annual reports (1941–1954); IMF annual reports and 1954 stand-by documentation; IBRD loan reports; Foreign Relations of the United States (1946–1947); the Cosío Villegas–Urquidi memorandum (Urquidi archive); addresses by Ramón Beteta and Antonio Carrillo Flores at the national banking conventions; macroeconomic series from Sergio Martín's Base de Datos Histórica (1933–1982).

**A Qualitative Approach to Household Memories and Social Mobility during the 1994–1995 Mexican Crisis**
{: .paper__title}

*Chapter in [A 30 años de la crisis financiera de 1994-1995: Experiencias y aprendizajes en la política, la academia y los hogares](https://ceey.org.mx/a-30-anos-de-la-crisis-financiera-de-1994-1995/){:target="_blank"}. <details><summary>Abstract</summary>
<div class="abstract" markdown="1">

**The book.** A volume produced by CEEY and the Center for Economic Studies at El Colegio de México marking thirty years of the 1994–1995 Mexican financial crisis. Its nine chapters, derived from three roundtables held at El Colegio de México in 2025, compile lessons from the policymakers who managed the episode and the academics who studied it, covering Mexico's representation at the IMF and the emergency package, financial liberalization and bank capital before the shock, the collapse of the exchange-rate band, and the political economy of the bank rescue. Three lessons recur: credibility, communication and macro consistency are inseparable; prudential supervision costs far less than a rescue; and stabilization cannot be separated from household outcomes.

**The chapter.** Co-authored with Alice Krozer, Roberto Vélez-Grajales and Raymundo M. Campos-Vázquez, the closing chapter examines the space between the exchange-rate shock and ex post stabilization, where households improvise. Drawing on five focus groups in Mexico City with two cohorts (providers in 1994 and those who experienced the crisis as students or new entrants), it traces the sequence from the 1991–1994 boom to the rupture and its persistent marks, including occupational changes that became permanent, lower income ceilings, eroded wealth, and shifts between private and public schooling. Recovery was systematically incomplete and mediated by family networks and informal finance rather than by the institutional response, suggesting that crises are not only interrupted trajectories but inherited ones.

</div>

**Output Gap and the Financial Cycle: A Bayesian Finance-Neutral Estimation with Fiscal, Monetary, and Macroprudential Applications for Mexico, 1980–2025**
{: .paper__title}

*MPhil thesis, El Colegio de México, 2026. Advisor: Gerardo Esquivel.*
{: .paper__meta}

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>
<div class="abstract" markdown="1">

Conventional output gap estimates ignore the financial cycle, and their real-time and ex-post versions diverge sharply. Both problems make them understate overheating during the booms that precede crises. I estimate a finance-neutral output gap for Mexico (1980–2025) in a Bayesian state-space framework in which financial variables determine the cyclical component, following Borio, Disyatat and Juselius (2013) and Amador-Torres et al. (2016). Six financial variables enter in a domestic block (credit, equity, housing, real interest rate) and an external block (real exchange rate, commodity prices), and the sample spans the 1994 domestic banking crisis, the 2008 global financial shock, and the 2020 non-financial shock as a control. The dominant channels are the real exchange rate and equity prices, not credit, consistent with Mexico's low credit penetration. In a real-time exercise that re-estimates the entire model each quarter using only information available at that date, the finance-neutral gap tracks its ex-post estimate far more closely than the HP filter in the eight quarters before the 1994 and 2008 crises (r = 0.86 vs. 0.21) and signals both episodes twelve quarters ahead. Substituting it into a cyclically adjusted fiscal balance, a Taylor rule, and the Basel III countercyclical buffer would have implied materially tighter policy during both build-ups.

</div>
</details>

**Distributive Effects of Recent Economic Crises in Mexico: A DiNardo-Fortin-Lemieux Decomposition of Growth Incidence Curves**
{: .paper__title}

*Working paper, 2025.*
{: .paper__meta}

*Draft in progress. Based on the paper submitted to the Premio Ifigenia Martínez.*

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>
<div class="abstract" markdown="1">
The distributive profile of an economic crisis is an empirical question that depends on the shock, the productive structure, and the policy response. This note compares Mexico's two most recent crises using ENIGH microdata, growth incidence curves, and the DiNardo–Fortin–Lemieux semiparametric decomposition. A weighted probit on the pooled sample generates reweighting factors that separate the change in the income distribution into shifts in household composition and shifts in returns to those characteristics. The 2008 crisis was regressive, with losses of roughly 8 % at the bottom against 4 % at the top. The 2020 pandemic traced an inverted U, with both tails contracting near 10–11 % while the middle lost 7–8 %. Despite these opposite profiles, a single channel dominates. The composition effect is positive in every percentile in both crises (+2 % to +3 %), while returns to characteristics collapsed and reproduce the shape of the total decline. Income fell not because the workforce became less educated or less skilled, but because what the economy paid for the same characteristics deteriorated, placing the labor market at the center of any policy response.
</div>
</details>

**The Modern Lender of Last Resort: An Analysis of the Evolving Role of Central Banks as Liquidity Providers in Times of Crisis**
{: .paper__title}

*BSc thesis, Faculty of Economics, UNAM, 2024. Advisor: Carlo Panico.*

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>
<div class="abstract" markdown="1">
This thesis traces the historical evolution of the lender of last resort function of central banks, from its conceptual origins to its exercise during the Global Financial Crisis. Using a historical-institutional approach based on documentary review and statistical evidence, it examines three episodes: the Bank of England in the nineteenth century, read through Baring, Thornton and Bagehot; the Federal Reserve during the Great Depression, whose preventive legislative design and decentralized structure constrained its response; and the post-2007 interventions of the Fed, the Bank of England and the ECB, compared measure by measure against the classical doctrine. The central finding is that the function follows no fixed prescription; it adapts to the institutional setting and to the specific features of each crisis. The modern lender of last resort has assumed markedly broader responsibilities (rate cuts, asset purchases, dedicated liquidity facilities, foreign-currency provision, market-making), with implications for moral hazard, too-big-to-fail, stigma, constructive ambiguity and international cooperation.
</div>
